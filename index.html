The oldest game we forgot how to play

A serpent older than the pyramids

Mehen was played in Egypt more than five thousand years ago, its board a stone serpent coiled tail-to-head. Then it vanished. For most of a century, museums could only guess at its rules by borrowing them from a much later African game.

In 2024, archaeologist James F. R. Masters proposed a different reconstruction: no dice at all, but a game of nerve and bluff, where marbles are hidden in a closed fist and a rival must guess how many. This app plays Mehen the way Masters imagined it — the first time in millennia the snake has moved again.

𓂀 No dice. No luck to hide behind. Only what you dare to conceal — and what your opponent dares to guess.

How to play

A single turn is three small moments. The whole game is these, again and again, up the serpent's back.

01

Conceal

The hider secretly closes one to five marbles inside a fist.

02

Guess

The rival names a number — trying to read the bluff.

03

March or pounce

Guess wrong and the hider advances a pharaoh. Guess right and the rival moves instead — or looses a lion.

To win: lead every one of your pharaohs up the coils and into Ra at the center. No exact count is needed — any move that would carry a piece past the end sends it home to the sun.

A note from the record

Lost, then listened to again

The Ashmolean Museum in Oxford keeps a Mehen board reckoned at more than 5,300 years old.

For nearly a century its rules were reconstructed from a much later Sudanese game called Hyena. In 2024, James F. R. Masters overturned that reading — arguing the game used no dice, and that its lion pieces were markers rather than players. The marble-guessing turn you play here is his proposed reconstruction. The true, original rules remain lost to history; this is our best and most careful guess at how the snake once moved.
